Supreme Court and the SIR: An Analysis of Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India

15 Oct, 2026

This is a Open Access copy of Ujjwal Kumar Singh1 and Anupama Roy2’s article Supreme Court and the SIR: An Analysis of Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India

Abstract

This article examines the Supreme Court’s judgement in Association for Democratic Reforms & Others v. Election Commission of India delivered on 27 May 2026 as a post facto validation of the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of the electoral roll undertaken by the Election Commission of India (ECI). We argue that by affirming an exceptional exercise of preparing the electoral roll de novo, the Supreme Court has interpreted the ECI’s constitutional mandate in a way which has the potential to set boundaries to the right of citizens to vote. By deciding that the ongoing modality of revising the electoral roll is constitutional, the Supreme Court has confirmed the mutation of a process designed to give material form to the democratic ideal of universal suffrage into a politically charged practice. In this shift, the electoral roll has been transformed into a political artefact embedded in a politics of non-citizenship and banal statism, whereby the political regime manoeuvres the bureaucratic apparatus of the state to make the right to vote dependent on identification regimes devised by the state.

Keywords: Electoral roll, Special Intensive Revision, Supreme Court of India, Election Commission of India, citizenship, documentary regimes

Introduction

A Supreme Court (SC) Bench of Chief Justice of India (CJI), Justice Surya Kant and Justice Joymala Bagchi, delivered the judgement in Association for Democratic Reforms and Others v. Election Commission of India (henceforth ADR) on 27 May 2026. The constitutionality of Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of the electoral roll (ER) in Bihar was challenged in the SC by the Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) and others following the Election Commission of India’s (ECI’s) announcement of SIR in Bihar on 24 June 2025. The hearings started on 10 July 2025 and concluded on 29 January 2026, with the court reserving its judgement. By this time, a new government was installed in Bihar in the state assembly election based on an ER prepared through SIR.

The SC, as Devadasan argues, was not ’evasive’. It heard the SIR challenge ‘actively’ but ‘blended’ the question of legality with that of SIR’s proper implementation, refraining from ‘differentiating’ and ‘sequencing’ the two. By deferring the judicial scrutiny of SIR’s legality, the court chose a route of ‘dialogic review’, examining the questions of accountability and transparency of the process (Devadasan, 2026) while simultaneously facilitating it through interim orders, easing its extension across the country. The SC hearings and interim orders reveal the procedural uncertainties that informed SIR in Bihar in the first phase and in West Bengal and other states/UTs in the second and third phases of its implementation.

In this article, we examine the SIR judgement as a verdict in a public interest litigation (PIL), validating the SIR post facto by affirming the arguments made in the SC by the ECI.3 In the past, PILs concerning the conduct of elections have resulted in the expansion of citizens’ voting rights through an innovative reading of the right to freedom of expression as encompassing the ‘citizen’s right to know’ construed as integral to the right to vote (see People’s Union for Civil Liberties (PUCL) & Another v. Union of India, 2003; Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms & Another, 2002). In 2018, in response to a PIL, the SC urged Parliament to enact a law to ensure that persons facing serious criminal charges do not enter public life (Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India, 2018). It made it mandatory for political parties to publish pending criminal cases against their candidates within 72 hours of selection of the candidate, explaining why they were chosen to contest the election.

The SC considered appeals for transparency in the electoral space through disclosure of antecedents of candidates to be in harmony with the ECI’s constitutional mandate to conduct elections. It interpreted this mandate as a ‘reservoir of power’, which gave the ECI scope to intervene in a variety of situations, not necessarily where law was silent (see, e.g., Mohinder Singh Gill v. The Chief Election Commissioner, 1978; Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms & Another, 2002; Vineet Narain and Others v. Union of India and Another, 1998). In the SIR judgment, however, the SC reinforced the ECI’s mandate in a way which arrays the ECI’s claims over unfettered powers to revise the ER through a special procedure, against the ‘people’s’ plea that it had the potential to circumscribe their right to vote.

Based on this, we make the larger argument that the SC’s initial deferral of a decision, as subsequent validation of SIR’s legality is a performance of modus vivendi rather than entrenchment of constitutional values,4 resulting in the affirmation of a process through which the ECI has become part of the state space (Brenner et al., 2003). By inserting itself in the governmental/administrative state space, the ECI has undermined its potential as a fourth branch institution contributing to the resilience of constitutional democracy (Katju, 2023; Khaitan, 2025; Tushnet, 2021). It also manifests the perils of ‘weak entrenchment’ of the ECI’s independence from the executive in the constitutional architecture (Bhatia, 2025) despite the Constituent Assembly’s (CA) preference for an autonomous ECI.

While this article focuses specifically on the SIR judgement, through this examination we hope to contribute to the scholarship on the ECI and electoral governance in India (Katju, 2023; Kumar, 2026; Sharma, 2018; Singh & Roy, 2019; Sridharan & Vaishnav, 2017), and to works on public institutions that construe the political field within which institutions function as constituting a matrix of power (Kapur & Mehta, 2005). We make these arguments across three sections, focussing on distinct aspects of the SIR judgement:

  1. Examining the modalities through which the SC affirms the SIR process in a judicial space activated by PIL;
  2. The SC’s endorsement of ECI’s power to verify the citizenship status of voters as an inquisitorial exercise; and
  3. The ramifications of the culture of authority (Klare, 1998; Langa, 2006) embodied in the judgement, on citizen’s right to vote.

The Judgement as an Authoritative Interpretive Voice

The ECI announced SIR in Bihar on 24 June 2025, invoking its constitutional powers under Article 324 of the Constitution to prepare the ER and its statutory authority under Section 21 of the Representation of the People Act 1950 (RPA) to decide the modality of revising it. Section 21(2) RPA requires summary revision every year and special summary revision in an election year, while Section 21(3) gives discretionary powers to ECI to undertake ‘intensive’ revision in ‘any’ constituency. The SIR is, however, unprecedented since it is an exercise to prepare the ER of the entire country afresh.

Presenting the reasons for this ‘special’ revision (rapid urbanisation, frequent migration, removal of dead voters, inclusion of young voters, and deletion of illegal migrants),5 the ECI’s instructions to the Chief Election Officer (CEO) of Bihar made it clear that SIR was an exercise to determine the eligibility of the entire electorate across the country, while ensuring that no eligible voter was excluded.6 Indeed, since Article 326 of the Constitution provides voting rights to Indian citizens only, the ECI considered it a constitutional obligation to ensure that only Indian citizens were part of the ER.7 Presenting its final arguments before the SC on 27 January 2026, its counsel clarified that the ECI does not ‘determine’ citizenship; it only ‘verifies’ eligibility, with ’no other consequences except non-inclusion in the electoral roll’.8

In its submissions before the SC and its instructions regarding SIR procedure, the ECI claimed that SIR was not unprecedented, and was conducted earlier in the ’entire country or parts of it’.9 This claim was contested by the petitioners who asserted that SIR’s procedures were unprecedented, exceptional, and unnecessary (Association for Democratic Reforms & Others v. ECI, 2025). Evidence showed that, unlike SIR, the 2003 revisions, declared the base year for SIR in Bihar, were undertaken within the purview of Section 21(2) and (3) of RPA (Devadasan, 2025).

The assumption that everyone is a non-voter and must prove their eligibility through documentary evidence establishing their relationship to a person in a prior voter list by filling enumeration forms, or risk removal from the ER, the petitioners argued, violated ECI’s own Manual of Electoral Rolls published in 2023 (Yadav, 2025). The Manual prescribes ‘detailed, sensitive and fair procedures’ for revising the ER for a ’truly universal franchise’, asking officials to pay ‘very special attention to any significant deletion’.10

In the SC hearings, the petitioners considered SIR an ‘unauthorised parallel mechanism’ going beyond Section 21(3) of RPA, which they argued did not authorise the ECI ’to ignore the “occupied field” of established rules’. The ECI, however, considered the phrase ‘as it may deem fit’ in Section 21(3) RPA, providing it a ‘broad statutory mandate’ reinforced by Article 324 of the Constitution, to ‘purify the electoral rolls through methods not restricted by standard rules’.

The petitioners contended that the SIR prescribed a procedure which was neither reasonable nor fair, leading to deletion of 6.5 million voters in Bihar, who were not served notices prior to their deletion. This was contrary to ‘substantive due process’, as laid down in the SC’s judgement in Lal Babu Hussein v. Electoral Registration Officer (1995). The ECI responded, however, that it followed ‘procedure established by law’—in this case Section 21(3) RPA—which alone was the standard to determine whether the procedure was valid in law (Rai, 2026).

While refusing to stay the revision, the judges understood their scrutiny of SIR as ’not merely’ probing into the ‘mechanics’ of revision but examining whether ’the constitutional promise of universal adult franchise’ was preserved through an ‘accurate, inclusive, and legally valid’ ER (ADR, para 7, p. 6). The SC accepted the petitions on the grounds that they raised an ‘important question’ that went ’to the root of the democratic process’ in the country, that is, the right to vote. It delineated three points for adjudication:

  1. Whether the SIR was in conformity with law;
  2. Whether the ECI had the authority to revise the ER through SIR; and
  3. Considering that the Bihar assembly elections were just a few months away, whether the time for completing the exercise was a constraint.11

In its judgement,12 the SC validated the SIR, affirming the ECI’s claims to exceptional powers of revising the ER. Refusing to see the SIR procedure as ‘disputed’, the judges argued that the ECI was obligated ’to ensure the purity, accuracy, and integrity’ of the ER—which required ‘appropriate procedures for verification, including the nature and extent of documentation required to establish eligibility’ (ADR v. ECI, p. 105). Affirming the use of discretionary powers to devise ‘special’ procedures for revision, the SC concluded that SIR was conducive to free and fair elections. The judgement prepared the grounds for bypassing the SC guidelines issued in Lal Babu Hussein (1995), which placed probative value on an existing ER for determining eligibility. It endorsed the ‘documentation regime’ installed by the SIR emulating the modus operandi of the National Register of Citizens (NRC) for ‘progeny tracing’ to verify citizenship.

The SIR verdict is not a terminal point in the contest over SIR, nor is it reducible to a legal document that states what is enforceable as law. The judgement, we argue, exemplifies the manner in which ‘authoritative interpretive communities’, for example, the judiciary (Baxi, 2008), often function by eschewing an immediate decision and refrain from laying down normative frames within which constitutional politics can make itself visible. As a ‘public interest’ petition, the SC was to be a different theatre of adjudication, driven not by the logic of enforcement but of deliberation, to evaluate the legitimacy of an executive action.

As a post-emergency phenomenon, the PIL transformed the SC into ‘a Supreme Court for Indians’ (Baxi, 1985a, p. 107) by making adjudication a people’s ally, bringing public power within the purview of judicial scrutiny. Through procedural innovations, it enabled a ‘conversation’ between the wielders of adjudicatory power and civil society (Baxi, 2000). PIL has, however, been criticised for spawning a mimetic politics of populism where the SC and the political executive make competing claims to representing ‘people’s interests’ (Baxi, 1985a; Bhuwania, 2016).

Hyper-Legality, Proceduralism, and Participation

We recall the debates on PIL to evaluate the SIR judgement within the frame of the contending relationship between legality and justice, and participation and proceduralism, to ask whether its hyper-legality dilutes the normative foundations of participation in democracy. The judgement situates political participation in the history of republicanism in Bihar. The judges extend their enquiry to ancient ‘Bihar’ not to buttress an argument for political concord, but to establish that the question how a polity ‘identifies those entitled to participate in government’ was actually an older one.

The Constitution, they argue, did not invent the ER. The idea of an ER specifying entitlement through eligibility, the judges argued, had always existed; with its constitutionalisation through universal adult franchise, it assumed a different character (ADR, p. 5). As ‘a legal record’ of the question that representative governments have always grappled with, that is, ‘identifying the persons entitled to participate in the choice of government’, the SIR embodied the governmental imperative of ‘first know[ing] whose votes may be counted’, before it counted them (ADR, p. 1).

The judgement frames political participation as a legal question rather than a normative one. This makes it difficult then to understand the relationship between the ER as an embodiment of popular sovereignty giving effect to the principle of universal adult franchise in a parliamentary democracy, and the nature of the vote in ancient republics as a ruling practice founded on the principle of participation by a few. Yet, as opening remarks in the judgement, they open up the possibility of thinking about the ER as a ‘historical’ document representing a sustained site of contest over membership and belonging, which are integral to the idea and practice of democratic citizenship.

The Faultlines and Judicial Activism

In the literature on judicial power and PIL, an activist judge is distinguished from an active judge on the basis of the potential they display to ‘disappoint the expectations of the governing elite’ (Baxi, 1985b) by fundamentally ‘recodifying/altering power relations in state and society’ (Khosla, 2009). This high threshold may not be achievable since the activist judge, even when serving as a ‘countermajoritarian’ check, functions ‘within the limits of the judicial process’, performing the function of both ‘stigmatising and legitimising the actions of governments’ (Sathe, 2003, p. 106).

While judicial decision-making has enhanced judicial power, extending it into areas where none had hitherto existed, e.g., judicial scrutiny of Parliament’s constituent powers and judicial appointments, the SC has also affirmed the ‘Constitution as power map’ as ‘an executive trusting’ document (Bhatia, 2025). Judicial decision-making in cases concerning the ECI’s administration of elections has been somewhat different. The SC has enhanced the ECI’s power over superintendence of elections by constraining Parliament’s authority to regulate elections through legislation (Article 327) by interpreting Article 324 as a ‘reservoir of powers’ wherever ’law [was] silent’. These interventions affirmed the ECI’s role as a fourth branch institution guaranteeing democracy and the rights of voters.

In ADR (2026), however, the court interprets the ECI’s powers under Article 324 as ‘plenary’ in nature, construing its ‘supervisory authority’ as inherently ’expansive’, ‘functioning as a continuous wellspring of power’ encompassing ’every facet and stage of the election machinery to ensure the sanctity of the electoral process’. Simultaneously, the judgement prefers to maintain a ‘constitutional equilibrium’ between the ECI and Parliament. To maintain this equilibrium, the court expected the ECI to exercise its ‘plenary’ powers under Article 324 with ‘due regard to parliamentary law’. Law, on the other hand, could illuminate the ECI’s ‘constitutional power’ but could not ’emasculate’ or ’extinguish’ its ‘overriding constitutional mandate to secure free and fair elections’ (ADR v. ECI, p. 44).

This delineation of ECI’s powers must be evaluated against the interpretation of Article 324 in cases (e.g., Anoop Baranwal) where the ECI’s autonomy vis-à-vis the executive has been under scrutiny. In March 2023, a Constitution Bench (CB) of the SC decided that the appointment of the CEC and other Election Commissioners would be made by a three-member committee comprising the Prime Minister, the leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and the CJI (Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India, 2023). By issuing this direction as an interim measure till Parliament enacted a law, the SC was not merely plugging a legislative vacuum. By tracing the constitutional history of Article 324 to the CA Debates (CAD), it was also delineating the norms and principles which would serve as standards for law-making. Any alteration in the guidelines would require justification through an argument stronger than those presented by the bench. The legislative repudiation of the SC’s guidelines did not offer any when Parliament enacted the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act in 2023.

Institutionalised Power

Through the SIR judgement, the SC has perhaps for the first time allied with the ECI to augment the institutionalised power of the state, making citizens’ right to vote subject to bureaucratic practices of political regimes. Significantly, the government’s defence of revision of ER through verification of citizenship is presented as an exercise of ‘purification’ with an emphasis on deletion to ensure that no ‘infiltrator’ sneaks into the electoral roll. In a discussion on electoral reforms in Parliament, dispelling ’the lies’ being perpetrated on SIR, the Home Minister explained that SIR was indispensable for preventing ‘infiltrators from electing Chief Ministers and Prime Ministers and thereby making the country insecure, purification of the voter list is essential’.13

It must be noted that a governmental activity of mapping the voting population is inherently different from any other governmental exercise of identification and enumeration. The objective of the former is affirmation of popular sovereignty and not the demonstration of stateness, that is, the capacity of the state to exercise authority over a territory and enforce an agreement over the terms of citizenship (Andersen et al., 2014). The ECI has constitutional responsibility under Article 324 and statutory authority under the RPA 1950 to prepare/revise the ER summarily or intensively, adopting modalities prescribed in law and giving reasons for adopting exceptional procedures. Procedural legality, however, is not sufficient if conditions of substantive reasonableness are not simultaneously met.

In Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952), the SC decided that the test of reasonableness must satisfy conditions of equality in Article 14 that provides equality before the law and equal protection of the law, protecting citizens/persons against discrimination by the state and also against arbitrary exercise of state power (The State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar Habib, 1952). In Naz Foundation (2009), the Delhi High Court went beyond the procedural test of reasonableness and laid down that the objectives of a law must be subjected to judicial scrutiny to confirm its adherence to constitutional morality, presenting ‘principles’ rather than ‘rules’ as standards to meet the requirement of ‘justice or fairness’ (Dworkin, 1967, p. 23).

In the SIR judgement, the SC endorsed ECI’s assumption of discretionary powers to revise the ER through an exceptional procedure and scrutinise the citizenship status of ‘persons seeking inclusion or continuation’ by putting in place a documentary regime imbricated in the enumeration process. The SC recognised the ECI’s obligation ’to ensure the purity, accuracy, and integrity’ of the ER as ‘foundational to the democratic process’, requiring ‘appropriate procedures for verification, including the nature and extent of documentation required to establish eligibility’ (ADR, p. 105). During the hearings, the ECI resisted making the list of documents ‘inclusive’ even when, at the behest of the SC, it permitted the submission of Aadhaar as an identity document. In its judgement, the SC upheld the ECI’s list arguing:

“[T]o hold otherwise would be to compel the Commission to adopt a lowest-common-denominator approach, thereby diluting the rigour of the verification exercise” (ADR, p. 108).

The validation of ECI’s authority to prepare an ER de novo through an inquisitorial enquiry involving verification of all names for ‘(re)entry’ into the ER, exemplifies the power of citation of an existing legal order (rather than morality) that gives binding character to the performance of judicial authority (Butler, 1993). Public interest cases are structured to allow judges to find out facts by placing people at its centre. Such ‘fact-finding’ enables judges to read new rights in the penumbra of existing ones (e.g., the right to know in the right to freedom of expression, right to privacy in the right to life) or, as in Nandini Sundar v. State of Chhattisgarh (2011), to articulate a distinctive constitutional vision. It was the specific iteration of ‘fraternity’ as a foundational constitutional principle in Nandini Sundar—extending its meaning beyond its perambulatory inscription—as a safeguard against unlimited state power and dehumanisation of citizens that was cited by the CB in October 2024 while dismissing the challenge to Section 6A of the Citizenship Act.14

Ramnath (2011) sees the importance of such judgements in their presentation of constitutional visions at different points in the history of the judiciary, and the multiple possibilities that reading the judgement by different audiences throws up—transforming the court into ‘a site for resistance and empowerment’. In the SIR judgement, while the judges framed their scrutiny as an enquiry into the relationship between the ER, universal adult franchise, and free and fair elections, by structuring their analysis through an adversarial template, they occluded the ‘sufferings’ of large numbers of legitimate voters who ran the risk of disenfranchisement.

Documentary Regimes of (Non)Citizenship

The CA envisaged the ECI as a body responsible for giving effect to the principle of equality of the citizen-voter by ensuring that no eligible voter is excluded from the ER on grounds of religion, race, caste, or gender. The preparation of the ER, unlike the preparation of a register of citizens (e.g., the NRC), is not an exercise for identifying citizens. However, whenever disputes over citizenship have emerged during the preparation/revision of the ER, the SC has resolved them by extending the ECI’s authority to ascertain citizenship. At the same time, however, the SC has prescribed guidelines for the procedures to be adopted by the ECI, especially when deleting names from the ER on grounds of non-citizenship.

In Inderjit Barua v. Election Commission of India (1984), the validity of the Assam legislative assembly election held in February 1983 was challenged on the grounds that the ER had not been properly revised before the election as required under the RPA. The petitioners also challenged the ECI’s instructions that asked electoral authorities not to delete names from the ER on grounds of ’lack of qualification of citizenship’. Citizenship, the ECI stated, could not be decided by electoral authorities (Inderjit Barua, 1984, p. 1). Validating the election and the ECI’s instructions, the SC considered the ECI’s directive as proceeding from the premise that:

“[T]hose whose names were already included in the previous electoral rolls should be prima facie regarded as satisfying the qualification of citizenship” (Inderjit Barua, 1984).

However, the SC authorised the ECI to decide in individual cases, whenever there was a contest or suspicion over the citizenship of those who claimed to be eligible to vote.

It was, however, in Lal Babu Hussein (1995), a case involving mass deletions, that the judges prescribed guidelines that the ECI must follow while deleting names. The judges were surprised that the ECI had deleted names en masse and issued notices asking for documentary evidence from largely poor and uneducated residents of slum clusters in Bombay and Delhi. They reminded the electoral officers to keep in mind, whenever the citizenship of a person whose name already figured in the ER was under suspicion, ’that the entire gamut for inclusion of the name in the electoral roll must have been undertaken and hence adequate probative value be attached to that factum before issuance of notice and in subsequent proceedings’ (Hussein, p. 11). With these procedural norms, the judges placed limits on ECI’s verification powers, which they argued flowed for a limited purpose from another law and were subject to provisions of the Constitution and the Citizenship Act. A decision to delete voters’ names must, they decided, meet the requirements of both ‘procedural fairness’ and ‘principles of natural justice’.

Bypassing Lal Babu Hussein

During the hearings, the petitioners referred to Lal Babu Hussein and argued that to give ‘adequate probative value’ to information provided by potential voters, the electoral officers must consider ‘documentary and other evidence’, since large numbers of the 47.4 million voters in Bihar were unlikely to possess the prescribed (government-issued) eligibility documents.15 According to the 2022 caste census in Bihar, only 20.47 per cent had a government job. As per the National Family Health Survey-3, only 2.8 per cent of Bihar’s population born between 2001 and 2005 has a birth certificate (Shastri, 2025).

The SIR judgement made Lal Babu Hussein guidelines irrelevant by distinguishing between an adjudicatory exercise ’triggered’ by a ‘specific dispute’ in which an existing entry in the ER would have a presumptive value, and a verification exercise being undertaken through the SIR. As a ‘verification’ exercise, the judges argue, the SIR was not concerned with individual disputes that could be resolved through ‘adjudication’. It required ‘a comprehensive re-examination of the [entire] electoral roll to ensure its accuracy, completeness, and integrity’ through an inquisitorial enquiry (ADR, p. 91):

When the Commission embarks upon such an exercise, it acts not as a mere adjudicator between competing claims but as a constitutional authority discharging a duty of systemic oversight. The constitutional power of superintendence, direction, and control over the preparation of electoral rolls, vested in the Commission, necessarily carries with it the authority to verify. (ADR, p. 91)

A Participatory ‘Documentation Regime’?

Rejecting the petitioner’s contention that non-inclusion in the draft roll results in final deletion, the judgement described the ER as ‘provisional’ by design, and its finalisation a ‘participatory’ process, which gave an opportunity to ’electors’ to ‘assert their entitlement, produce supporting material, and contest any proposed exclusion’ (ADR, p. 102). While the judges were inclined to see enrolment as a participatory process, the documentary regime spawned by the SIR process skewed the terms of participation in favour of the verifying authority, whose satisfaction alone gave legibility to voter-citizens.

The judges refer to ‘documentation regime’ as a condition which made the SIR effective as a verification process. In the literature on documentary practices of the state, a ‘documentary regime’ is founded in the ‘distinguishability assumption’ (Sadiq, 2009), that is, the belief that it is possible to distinguish between citizens and non-citizens on the basis of documents. The centrality given in SIR to documents as ‘proof of citizenship’ (Sadiq, 2009), quite like the NRC, inverts the relationship between documents and citizenship. Persons can be ‘verified’ as citizens only if they are able to insert themselves in an evidentiary paradigm that can establish a relationship between them and persons to whom their legacy can be traced.

The evidentiary paradigm spawned by a documentary regime works in a way where documents do not have veracity in isolation and acquire evidentiary worth only by becoming part of a serialised link in relationship with other documents. Documents discrete in their origin and purpose are sutured together to serve another purpose—to establish lineage and facilitate progeny tracing—changing the way in which documents such as a voter ID card or a passport were hitherto seen as having a stable relationship with citizenship.16

In its letter to the CEO of Bihar,17 the ECI noted its constitutional obligation under Article 326 to ensure that only Indian citizens are registered as voters. Following the NRC’s modus operandi, the SIR works on a presumption of non-citizenship, assuming that everyone is a non-voter and must prove their eligibility through documentary evidence—different for each category of ‘potential voters’—depending upon their date of birth:

  • Category 1: Those whose names were already on the ER of 2003: a copy of the 2003 roll would be sufficient;
  • Category 2: Those whose names were not on the 2003 roll born between 1 July 1987 and 2 December 2004: required to show two kinds of documentary proof establishing the date and place of birth of the claimant and one of their parents;
  • Category 3: Those born after 2 December 2004: faced a further burden of proof—their own date and place of birth, as well as the date and place of birth of both their parents.

These categories correspond to the amendments made in the citizenship law through 1985–1986, laying down that a person could be a citizen by birth only if one of their parents was an Indian citizen, and with the amendment in 2003, only if both parents were Indian citizens.

SIR as Vertical Citizenship

In their analysis of citizenship, the judges veer between the idea of citizenship as ‘a juridical status from which a constellation of rights, duties and entitlements flow’, suggesting a vertical relationship with the state, and the idea of citizenship as a practice inherent in the ‘architecture of representative democracy’ in which only citizens can participate (ADR, p. 114). They simultaneously, however, insert a relational content by associating citizenship with ‘recognition’ of an individual as a member of the constitutional order, consequential for ‘identity, belonging and legal personality’ (ADR, p. 114). A process that ’touched’ upon citizenship must, therefore, they argue, demonstrate a ‘high degree of procedural fairness and institutional restraint’ (ADR, p. 114).

Thus, the ECI’s constitutional responsibility to verify citizenship could not be one of determination of citizenship, ‘but only an enquiry into eligibility’. While the determination of citizenship was outside the ECI’s competence (ADR, p. 114), its power to determine the threshold of eligibility gave it scope for ’enquiry’. The judges at this point make what they call ‘a clear and principled distinction’ between ‘adjudication’ of citizenship amounting to a conclusive determination of status under the Citizenship Act, and ‘an administrative satisfaction as to eligibility for enrolment’, which was a ’limited enquiry’ for the purpose of ’electoral representation’ (ADR, p. 115). This enquiry could not be adjudicatory but inquisitorial since the ECI was empowered to ’examine questions bearing upon citizenship’ only for the purpose of determining ‘inclusion or exclusion’ from the ER.

The judges made ECI’s authority to verify citizenship subject to judicial review to ensure that the enquiry was conducted ‘in accordance with law and within the bounds of procedural fairness’ (ADR, p. 116). After laying down the standard for institutional behaviour and practice, in the following paragraph, however, they set a lower threshold for judicial scrutiny by declaring that ECI’s action of ‘declining enrolment or initiating action for deletion’ must be seen as ’necessarily prima facie and contextual’ and ‘in its proper perspective’, that is, the evidence furnished by an individual ‘did not inspire confidence’. This, however:

“[D]id not amount to a declaration that the individual is not a citizen of India; it merely reflects the commission’s inability to be satisfied, for electoral purposes, that the statutory conditions are met” (ADR, pp. 116–117).

A lower threshold for scrutiny comes from what the judges construe as ’limited’ consequences of non-inclusion, affecting only a person’s ‘right to participate in the electoral process’, and not divesting them of ‘claims of citizenship’, nor ‘foreclosing’ the determination of that question by a competent authority under the Citizenship Act (ADR, p. 117) within ‘a reasonable time frame’, but before upcoming elections at any level in the concerned constituency (ADR, p. 118). ECI’s limited powers, the judges believed, would preserve ’the constitutional balance’, enabling the ECI to discharge its duty of ‘preserving the purity and integrity of the electoral roll’ while respecting the statutory scheme governing citizenship (ADR, p. 118).

The judicial averment of the limited consequences of exclusion/deletion from the electoral roll has not been confirmed in practice. By the time the SIR judgement was delivered, the BJP governments in Bihar and West Bengal had declared that those deleted from the SIR, except those in West Bengal whose appeals were pending with tribunals, would not receive welfare benefits from the government.18 The politics of non-citizenship on which the SIR is premised appears to have consequences beyond election time.

‘A Continuing Right to Vote’

On 9 February 2026, hearing a plea against the ‘freezing’ of the ER, Justice Bagchi vocalised the need for a robust appellate mechanism and a ‘continuing right to vote’. Describing the right to vote as a ‘sentiment’ which took precedence over its constitutional and legal status, he assured the petitioners that the court had the voters’ interests in mind, and asked the appellate tribunals to work on the ‘principle of inclusion’ (Sinha, 2026). On the recommendation of the Chief Justice of the Calcutta High Court, on 20 March 2026, the SC notified 19 appellate tribunals presided over by retired judges of the High Court to consider appeals against deletions that occurred in the adjudication process. Invoking its constitutional powers under Article 142 that empower it to issue an order to ensure ‘complete justice’, the SC ordered the ECI to publish supplementary voter lists restoring those cleared by tribunals to the ER.19

The hearing of appeals by tribunals was initiated only days before the ECI froze the ER under the existing laws, followed by a reprieve by the SC, which allowed the de-freezing of the ER up to two days before the polling day for each phase. In its order of 13 April 2026, the SC noted that 3.4 million appeals had been filed.20 The numbers were huge and the days so few that a mere 139 names were added to the ER before the polling day in the first phase, and 1,468 names were restored before voting in the second phase. The appeals of the rest remained pending, and they could not vote.

Significantly, 65 electoral officers on poll duty found their names deleted from the ER in what they termed in their appeal to the SC as ’ex facie arbitrariness’, without a notice being sent to them before deletion. Justice Sivagnanam, a former Chief Justice of Calcutta High Court, who had been asked by the SC to undertake a special hearing of two cases, both of which he approved,21 resigned on 7 May 2026 after clearing 1,777 cases in 22 days, without rejecting any. He stated that at the pace at which the tribunals were proceeding, they would require four years to hear all the petitions.22

Hearing a petition filed by Mostari Banu against the SIR process in West Bengal (Mostari Banu v. Election Commission of India, 2025), Justice Bagchi expressed concern at the deviation in West Bengal from the SIR template followed in other states, and the lingering process of appeals before the appellate tribunals as the polling days approached. The judges declined, however, to issue directions to the tribunals, which would ‘disrupt’ the process. The Court’s approach, Justice Bagchi explained, was ’not to interdict’ but to ‘intervene’ to ‘promote fair elections’. A process which involved scrutiny at a large scale, they argued, would have inevitable errors, which by themselves would not justify ‘halting the electoral process’.23

Close to the polling day when the appellate tribunals had managed to hear only a minuscule percentage of claims against deletion, Justice Bagchi appeared helpless—a far cry from his assertion that the right to vote is a continuing right:

“[In] this election yes perhaps they can’t vote. The more valuable right to remain on the rolls shall be preserved”.24

The idea that the right to cast a vote could be deferred, and the voter left in limbo, till the time their claim to continue to exist on the ER is resolved through adjudication, absolves the ECI of the responsibility to ensure the ‘purity, accuracy and integrity’ of the ER before the electoral process has begun. In 2002, the ECI had concluded that assembly elections could not be held in Gujarat in the context of incomplete rolls and missing electorates after communal violence. Deciding the time of the polls, it emphasised, was within the ECI’s jurisdiction, and ensuring that ER is complete before it, was its responsibility.25

A Sentimental Right?

While the documentary regime and the ECI’s powers of determining eligibility as a citizenship verification exercise have hitherto structured the debates on SIR, it is important to frame the debate differently by placing the right to vote as a non-negotiable starting point. Initiating the discussion with the question—to what extent has the ECI succeeded in preserving the right to vote through the SIR—would compel a different approach to evaluating a deeply fraught exercise.

The right to vote in India is imbricated in the history of the anti-colonial movement for sovereign nationhood. A prior consensus on universal adult franchise existed in the CA drawing from the anti-colonial movement and swaraj as a constitutional future. As a democratic principle, it recognised the claims of each person to sovereign personhood, which became consequential for choosing parliamentary democracy as the preferred form of government.

Deeply complex and contending interpretations of the right to vote coexist at present, arrayed along positions that see franchise as a constitutional and/or a fundamental right, and those that see it as a right regulated by statutes. Amidst the SIR hearings in Mostari Banu’s appeal, Justice Bagchi’s opinion that the ‘right to remain on the roll and the right to vote’ in a country in which you are born are ’not just constitutional but also sentimental rights’,26 steers attention to ‘deprivation’ of the right to vote, and its ramifications for political personhood, citizenship, and belonging. This becomes especially significant in a context where deprivation of a place on the ER may result in suspicion over citizenship itself.

In cases dealing with the withdrawal of the legal status of citizenship, a number of SC judgements have affirmed that these processes must be subjected to ‘a rigorous standard of judicial review’ since citizenship is a ‘most precious right’ (State of Arunachal Pradesh v. Khudiram Chakma, 1993), which constitutes the foundation from which ‘critical human rights’ flow. The SC cautioned that ‘deprivation’ of rights leads to ‘powerlessness’, especially of ‘discrete or insular majorities’ to ‘protect themselves through the normal channels of the political process’ (Navtej Johar v. Union of India, 2018). These, the SC emphasised, must be protected by constitutional courts, by imposing a high burden upon the state to justify laws that are facially discriminatory.

In the SC, the ECI’s counsel assured that the SIR was not like the NRC.27 The ‘constitutional duty’ of the ECI to ‘determine’ citizenship was only for the purpose of registering a person as an elector. Yet, since the Constitution placed citizenship at ’the centre of the right to vote’, even when a statute might limit the ECI’s powers, the Constitution ‘continued to vest authority’ in it, in all election-related matters.28 The power to inquire into and ‘verify’ a voter’s citizenship was imbricated in the ECI’s authority; however, verification was only for the purpose of determining a person’s inclusion in the ER, not their ‘right to remain in India’ or an ‘order of deportation’.29

Yet, the apprehension expressed by the petitioners that the SIR was based on a system that ‘presumptively doubts a person’s citizenship’,30 was made manifest in its potential to create ‘graded citizenship’—where exclusion from the rolls and disenfranchisement would lead to forms of non-citizenship. It is worth recalling the relationship between civil, political, and social rights in T. H. Marshall’s (1950) analysis, where he sees political rights as strengthening civil rights by changing them from individual entitlements to rallying points for collective struggles for a fair share in society’s resources. The SIR seems to have set in a politics of enervation of not just political rights but also civil and social rights, attenuating the co-constitutive relationship between them.

Conclusions

The ongoing SIR process is exceptional for having adopted an unprecedented, extraordinary procedure exceeding the provisions of Section 21 of RPA 1950, which permits only summary and intensive revisions, to prepare afresh an ER for the entire country based on the presumption of non-citizenship. The ECI has justified the SIR procedure by claiming the existence of a ’necessity’ which is not covered by an existing law. The law, however, allows for its own subversion, by providing the ECI with discretionary authority to identify and declare the existence of exceptional conditions and assume the powers to address them. Both—the declaration of a pressing necessity and the assumption of powers to address it—result in not only the suspension of the existing legal order but also in transforming it into something else.31

The right to vote was considered by the sub-committee on fundamental rights as a ‘fundamental right of every citizen to choose in free, secret, and periodic elections’ (Rao, 2022, p. 460) and, in the nature of fundamental rights by Dr Ambedkar (p. 461). Its eventual inclusion in the chapter on elections made it dependent upon the administrative responsibility of the ECI to prepare the ER according to statutory procedures. The majority judgement in Anoop Baranwal (2023) urged that the ‘right to elect’, even if it was treated as a statutory right, could not be divorced from the mandate of Article 326, making it a right of the greatest importance and the foundation for a free and fair election. In his concurring judgement, Justice Rastogi preferred to see it as a fundamental right, which would raise the level of scrutiny of the working of the ECI.

By affirming the constitutionality of the SIR process, the SC has acceded to the transformation of a process that was envisaged in the constitution as giving material form to the democratic ideal of universal suffrage into a politically charged practice. Through this shift, the ER is transformed into a political artefact embedded in a politics of non-citizenship and banal statism (Jovanović, 2026), whereby the bureaucratic apparatus of the state is manoeuvred to make the right to vote dependent on identification regimes devised by political regimes.
